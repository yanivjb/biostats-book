## Study Design, "Power and Precision" Section (Tutor Knowledge Base)

**Context:**
This section connects study design to sampling error, precision, and power. It emphasizes that researchers cannot eliminate sampling error, but can contain it through better design: more reliable measurements, balanced designs, matching, blocking, appropriate handling of non-independence, and planned sample sizes.

---

### Learning Goals

By the end of this section, students should be able to:

1. Describe design strategies that decrease variability and increase precision.
2. Explain measurement reliability as a way to reduce noise.
3. Explain why balanced designs improve estimates of treatment differences.
4. Explain matching and blocking as strategies for removing irrelevant variation.
5. Recognize pseudoreplication and explain why the unit of analysis must match the unit of randomization.
6. Explain why small effects require disproportionately large samples.
7. Recognize that reducing variance can substitute for collecting more data.
8. Know that tools such as `pwr`, `presize`, and webapps can help plan sample size.

---

### Key Concepts and Definitions

* **Precision:** How tightly an estimate is pinned down.
* **Power:** The probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis.
* **Effect size:** The size of the relationship or difference the study is trying to detect.
* **Measurement reliability:** Consistency of measurement. More reliable measurements reduce noise.
* **Balanced design:** Similar sample sizes across treatment groups or comparison groups.
* **Matching:** Pairing similar units and comparing within pairs to cancel out irrelevant variation.
* **Blocking:** Grouping units by a nuisance factor and ensuring treatments are represented within each block.
* **Randomized Complete Block Design:** A blocked design where every block contains a complete, balanced set of treatment levels.
* **Split-plot design:** A design where one factor is randomized at a coarse scale and another at a finer scale.
* **Pseudoreplication:** Treating non-independent observations as if they were independent replicates.

---

### Conceptual Takeaways

* Sampling error cannot be stopped, but design can contain it.
* More data is not the only path to more power. Reducing irrelevant variation can be just as important.
* Balanced designs are especially important when estimating differences.
* Matching and blocking are helpful because they let the analysis account for known sources of variation.
* Blocking/matching and pseudoreplication are not contradictions. Grouping is useful when modeled and misleading when ignored.
* Small effects are expensive to detect because required sample size grows roughly with one over effect size squared.
* Researchers should choose a biologically meaningful effect size before planning power.

### Notes for Tutor Responses

If a student says "just increase sample size," ask:

> Is the problem too few independent units, too much irrelevant variation, or both?

If a student asks whether blocking creates pseudoreplication, try:

> The grouping is not the problem by itself. What happens if the analysis ignores that grouping?

If a student asks what effect size to use in power analysis, guide them back to biology:

> What is the smallest difference that would actually matter for the scientific question?

---

### Common Student Confusions

* Thinking power only means sample size.
* Thinking standard deviation of raw data and precision of an estimate are the same thing.
* Thinking balanced design is about fairness rather than precision.
* Thinking matching or blocking removes the need to model the design.
* Treating repeated measures on the same unit as independent samples.
* Thinking 80% power is a magic rule rather than a convention.
* Forgetting to plan for lost samples, dead plants, degraded RNA, dropouts, or missing data.

---

### Tutor Guidance

Keep power grounded in the biological question. Do not let students chase "significance" for its own sake. Ask what estimate or effect size would be informative.

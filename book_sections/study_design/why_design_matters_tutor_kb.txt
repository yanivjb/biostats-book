## Study Design, "What do you want to know?" Section (Tutor Knowledge Base)

**Context:**
This section introduces the chapter's central idea: before asking how to analyze data, researchers must ask what question they want the study to answer. The section distinguishes scientific models from statistical models and uses the Clarkia petal-size and hybridization study to show how a biological hypothesis becomes a set of design decisions.

---

### Learning Goals

By the end of this section, students should be able to:

1. Distinguish a scientific model from a statistical model.
2. Explain why statistical models do not automatically answer biological questions.
3. Translate a scientific question into concrete design choices.
4. Identify the separate design decisions of who to measure, what to measure, where to measure, how many replicates to include, and what assignment or comparison is possible.
5. Recognize that a study does not need a perfect statistical plan before it begins, but it does need a viable path from design to analysis.

---

### Key Concepts and Definitions

* **Scientific model:** A simplified understanding of how the world works, grounded in biology or another science. It explains observations and makes predictions.
* **Statistical model:** A mathematical description of patterns in data. It knows nothing about biology unless the researcher designs the study and interprets the model in biological context.
* **Study design:** The set of decisions made before analysis: what population or units enter the study, what gets measured, where and when measurements happen, how treatments or comparisons are created, and how much replication is needed.
* **Response variable:** The outcome the study tries to explain. In the Clarkia RIL example, this is the proportion of hybrid seeds.
* **Explanatory variable:** The predictor or feature used to explain the response. In the Clarkia example, this includes petal area, with petal color, location, and other traits also relevant.

---

### Conceptual Takeaways

* The first design question is not "what test should I run?" It is "what do I want to know?"
* A statistical model can describe a pattern, but the study design determines whether the pattern addresses the scientific question.
* Biological hypotheses often need to be sharpened into measurable variables and fair comparisons.
* Design choices are compromises. The goal is not perfection, but a design good enough to inform the question.
* It is acceptable to know that future tools, such as multiple regression or mixed-effect models, will be needed, as long as the study has a viable analysis path.

### Notes for Tutor Responses

If a student asks why scientific and statistical models are different, do not start with a long definition. Try:

> What is each model trying to do: explain how biology works, or summarize patterns in data?

If a student asks whether the RIL study proves petal area causes hybridization, guide them toward the design limitation:

> What would need to be randomly assigned for that causal claim to be clean?

---

### Common Student Confusions

* Thinking a statistical model is automatically a scientific explanation.
* Thinking a significant slope proves the biological mechanism.
* Treating "what to measure" as obvious rather than as a design decision.
* Forgetting that proxies, locations, replicates, and units all shape the claim.
* Thinking a study must be perfect before it is worth doing.

---

### Tutor Guidance

Keep returning to the sentence: "What claim can this design support?" Help students name the claim, then inspect whether the measured variables and comparison structure actually support it.

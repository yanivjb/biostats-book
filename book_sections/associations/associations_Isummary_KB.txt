## Section: Summarizing Associations I — Summary & Practice

---

# Section Context

This section reviews the core ideas introduced in the **Summarizing Associations I** chapter and provides practice questions.

Students should consolidate their understanding of how to describe relationships between variables using:

* conditional proportions
* conditional means
* difference in means
* Cohen’s d

The emphasis remains on **describing patterns in data**, not testing hypotheses or establishing causation.

---

# Core Ideas from the Chapter

### Associations describe patterns

An **association** occurs when the value of one variable tends to differ depending on the value of another variable.

Associations describe **patterns in observed data**, not causal relationships.

---

### Conditional summaries

Many association summaries are **conditional summaries**.

These describe a variable **within a group defined by another variable**.

Examples:

```id="s1"
P(visit | pink)
mean(prop_hybrid | visited)
```

Conditional summaries allow comparisons across groups.

---

### Associations between two categorical variables

When both variables are categorical, associations are summarized using:

**Conditional proportions**

Example:

* probability of visitation among pink flowers
* probability of visitation among white flowers

Visualization tools include:

* stacked bar plots
* proportional bar plots
* grouped bar plots

---

### Associations between categorical and numeric variables

When comparing a numeric variable across groups, associations are summarized using:

**Conditional means**

Example:

* average hybrid seed proportion among plants receiving visits
* average hybrid seed proportion among plants receiving no visits

---

### Difference in means

A simple summary of group differences is the **difference between conditional means**.

Conceptually:

```id="s2"
difference in means =
mean(Y | group1)
-
mean(Y | group2)
```

---

### Cohen’s d

Cohen’s d standardizes the difference in means by the **pooled standard deviation**.

It expresses group differences in units of **standard deviations**.

Students should understand that Cohen’s d measures **effect size**, not statistical significance.

---

# Decision Guide for Students

When analyzing a relationship between variables, students should first identify the **variable types**.

### Step 1 — Identify variable types

Ask:

* Are both variables categorical?
* Is one categorical and one numeric?

---

### Step 2 — Choose the appropriate summary

```id="s3"
categorical + categorical → conditional proportions
categorical + numeric → conditional means
```

---

### Step 3 — Visualize the relationship

Common plots include:

For categorical–categorical:

* stacked bar plots
* proportional bar plots

For categorical–numeric:

* boxplots
* jittered scatterplots
* histograms or density plots

---

### Step 4 — Interpret carefully

Students should ask:

* How large is the difference between groups?
* Could other variables explain the pattern?
* Does the data support causal conclusions?

Associations alone **do not establish causation**.

---

# Common Student Difficulties

### Confusing counts with proportions

Students sometimes compare **bar heights representing counts** when the meaningful comparison is **proportions**.

Encourage students to focus on **conditional probabilities**.

---

### Ignoring variability

Students may focus only on mean differences and overlook:

* spread
* skew
* overlapping distributions

Visualization helps reveal these patterns.

---

### Misinterpreting conditional notation

Students sometimes reverse conditional probabilities.

Example:

```id="s4"
P(visit | pink)
```

means:

> probability of visitation **given pink flowers**

not the reverse.

---

# Tutor Guidance for Practice Questions

When students ask about practice questions:

1. Do **not give the final answer directly**.
2. Ask guiding questions that help them reason through the problem.
3. Encourage them to interpret **plots and summaries**.
4. Suggest computing conditional summaries in R if helpful.

Example tutor response style:

> “What type of variables are you comparing here? Once you identify that, you can decide whether you should be looking at conditional proportions or conditional means.”

---

# Typical Student Questions

Students commonly ask:

* “Which summary statistic should I use here?”
* “Is this comparing proportions or means?”
* “What does Cohen’s d tell us?”
* “How do I interpret this plot?”
* “Does this pattern imply causation?”

The tutor should guide students toward **interpreting patterns and choosing appropriate summaries**, rather than simply calculating answers.

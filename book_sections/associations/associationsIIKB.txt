# Core Concepts

## Association

An **association** describes how two variables vary together.

Associations may appear as:

* variables increasing together
* one variable increasing while another decreases
* differences across groups
* clustering patterns in data

Association **does not imply causation**.

Understanding associations helps scientists generate hypotheses about biological mechanisms.

---

# Covariance

Covariance is a numerical summary describing how two variables vary together.

Positive covariance
→ large values of one variable tend to occur with large values of the other.

Negative covariance
→ large values of one variable tend to occur with small values of the other.

Covariance near zero
→ little or no linear association.

---

## Two Equivalent Definitions of Covariance

### Product-of-means formulation

[
Cov(X,Y) = \overline{XY} - \bar{X}\bar{Y}
]

This compares the **mean of the products** to the **product of the means**.

---

### Cross-product formulation

[
Cov(X,Y) = \frac{\sum (X_i-\bar{X})(Y_i-\bar{Y})}{n-1}
]

Steps:

1. Compute each variable’s deviation from its mean
2. Multiply the deviations
3. Sum the products
4. Divide by (n-1)

The products of deviations are called **cross-products**.

Covariance is the **average cross-product of deviations**.

---

# Relationship Between Covariance and Variance

Variance is a special case of covariance.

[
Var(X) = Cov(X,X)
]

This means variance measures how a variable varies **with itself**.

Recognizing this connection helps students understand covariance more intuitively.

---

# Correlation

Correlation standardizes covariance so it becomes easier to interpret.

[
r = \frac{Cov(X,Y)}{s_X s_Y}
]

Correlation has three key properties:

1. It ranges from **-1 to 1**
2. It is **unitless**
3. It measures **strength of linear association**

Interpretation:

* **r ≈ 1** → strong positive linear relationship
* **r ≈ -1** → strong negative linear relationship
* **r ≈ 0** → little or no linear relationship

Correlation measures **linear association**, not general association.

---

# Important Interpretation Principles

### Correlation does not imply causation

A strong correlation may arise because:

* one variable causes another
* reverse causation
* a third variable (confounding)
* coincidence or chance

Students should be encouraged to think about **biological mechanisms**, not just statistical patterns.

---

# Visualization of Associations

The primary visualization tool for two numeric variables is the **scatterplot**.

Each point represents one observation.

Scatterplots allow students to:

* detect patterns
* evaluate linear trends
* identify outliers
* assess whether correlation is appropriate

---

# ggplot Visualization

In ggplot2:

Scatterplots use:

```r
geom_point()
```

Trend lines can be added with:

```r
geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE)
```

This line **guides the eye** and helps visualize the direction of association.

The line summarizes the overall linear pattern but does **not imply causation**.

---

# Interpreting Scatterplots

Students should be able to distinguish:

### Strength of association

How tightly points cluster.

### Linear association

How closely points follow a straight-line pattern.

### Nonlinear association

Variables may have strong relationships even if correlation is small.

---

# Common Student Confusions

Students often:

### Confuse covariance and correlation

Covariance depends on scale; correlation does not.

---

### Assume correlation implies causation

Encourage students to consider biological explanations and confounding.

---

### Assume correlation measures all relationships

Correlation measures **linear relationships only**.

Strong nonlinear relationships can have low correlations.

---

### Confuse prediction with correlation

Correlation describes association but does not guarantee accurate prediction.

# Key R Functions

Students may use:

`cov()`
Calculates covariance between two numeric variables.

`cor()`
Calculates correlation between two numeric variables.

`group_by()`
Groups observations before summarizing.

`summarise()`
Calculates summary statistics such as means, covariance, and correlation.

`geom_point()`
Creates scatterplots.

`geom_smooth()`
Adds a trend line to a scatterplot.

By the end of this chapter students should be able to:

* recognize different types of associations between variables
* calculate covariance and correlation
* interpret covariance and correlation conceptually
* visualize numeric associations with scatterplots
* connect statistical summaries to biological questions
* recognize that association alone does not establish causation

# Tutor Guidance

When students ask questions:

* begin by asking them what they notice in the data or plot
* guide them to interpret associations before computing statistics
* avoid giving direct quiz answers
* encourage them to explore examples using simple datasets

Examples students may use include:

* `penguins`
* `iris`
* their own experimental datasets

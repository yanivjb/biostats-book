## Section: Two Categorical Variables

---

# Section Context

This section introduces how to **describe associations between two categorical variables**.

Students begin by reviewing **unconditional proportions** and then learn how to compute **conditional proportions** to compare outcomes across groups.

The key conceptual progression is:

1. Start with **unconditional proportions**.
2. Introduce **conditional proportions**.
3. Compare conditional proportions across groups.
4. Describe **deviations from independence** as evidence of association.

This section focuses on **description and intuition**, not formal statistical testing.

---

# Learning Goals

By the end of this section students should be able to:

1. Calculate and interpret **conditional proportions**.
2. Visualize associations between two categorical variables.
3. Explain the **multiplication rule for independent events**.
4. Recognize when data **deviate from independence**.
5. Interpret contingency table summaries and bar plots.

---

**petal_color**

**visited**

* no_visits

Students explore whether **pollinator visitation differs between pink and white flowers**.

# Key Concept: Unconditional Proportions

An unconditional proportion describes the **overall frequency** of a category in the dataset.

Example:

Proportion of flowers that received a pollinator visit.

This summary ignores any grouping variables.

Students often visualize unconditional proportions using **a stacked bar plot**.

---

# Key Concept: Conditional Proportions

A conditional proportion describes the probability of an outcome **within a specific group**.

Example:

```
P(Visit | Pink)
P(Visit | White)
```

These represent:

* probability of receiving a pollinator visit **given pink flowers**
* probability of receiving a pollinator visit **given white flowers**

Conditional proportions allow students to **compare groups**.

---

# Visualizing Conditional Proportions

Associations between categorical variables are commonly visualized with **bar plots**.

Useful display options include:

**Stacked bar plots**

Show counts within groups.

**Fill bar plots**

Show proportions within groups.

Students should learn to interpret both.

---

# Key Concept: Independence

Two categorical variables are **independent** if the probability of observing both events equals the product of their individual probabilities.

Mathematically:

```
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
```

Example:

```
P(visited AND pink)
=
P(visited) × P(pink)
```

If this equality holds, the variables are **independent**.

---

# Key Concept: Deviation from Independence

If the observed joint probability differs from the expected value under independence, the variables show **association**.

Example structure:

```
Observed:
P(visited AND pink)

Expected under independence:
P(visited) × P(pink)
```

If these values differ, the data show **association between the variables**.

This deviation provides the intuition that will later connect to **covariance**.

---

# Common Student Confusions

### Confusing counts and proportions

Students often compare bar heights representing **counts** when the meaningful comparison is **proportions**.

Encourage students to ask:

> “Am I comparing totals or probabilities?”

---

### Forgetting the conditioning

Students may misinterpret

```
P(Visit | Pink)
```

as the probability of pink given visits.

Reinforce the idea that **the condition appears after the vertical bar**.

---

### Assuming association implies causation

Even if visitation differs between flower colors, this does **not prove that color causes visitation**.

Other factors could influence the pattern.

---

# Tutor Guidance

When helping students:

1. Ask them to identify the **two variables being compared**.
2. Ask which variable is treated as the **conditioning variable**.
3. Encourage students to interpret **plots before computing statistics**.
4. Reinforce the difference between **counts and proportions**.

Keep responses brief and guide students toward **interpreting the biological meaning of patterns**.
